When Should You Start Applying for Scholarships?

A grade-by-grade guide to when students can begin researching scholarships, preparing materials, and submitting applications.

By Annabelle Klosterman · Scholarship guidance for students and families

The best time to start looking for scholarships is earlier than many students expect. That does not mean a middle school student needs to spend hours applying for awards. It means students and families can build awareness, collect experiences, and learn how the process works before deadlines become urgent.

Start researching early, then apply when you meet the eligibility rules. Every scholarship has its own timeline, so always check the sponsor’s requirements.

Middle school: explore interests and build habits

At this stage, focus on exploring activities, interests, and possible career paths. Keep a simple record of projects, responsibilities, community involvement, creative work, and accomplishments. Students do not need to build a résumé just for scholarships, but noticing what they enjoy and contribute can make later applications easier.

9th and 10th grade: learn what opportunities exist

Students can begin learning about local and national scholarship opportunities, especially programs with multi-year involvement or early eligibility. Keep track of activities and meaningful experiences. Start noticing the kinds of questions scholarship essays ask, but do not feel pressured to apply before you qualify.

11th grade: build a scholarship plan

Junior year is a useful time to research scholarship sources, create a tracker, and identify opportunities that may open during senior year. Draft a basic activities list, think about potential recommenders, and practice writing about experiences. Some scholarships are open to juniors, so check eligibility rather than assuming every award is for seniors.

12th grade: manage applications and deadlines

Senior year is often the busiest scholarship period. Make a calendar of deadlines, gather transcripts and recommendation requirements, and work on essays early. Do not wait until a college admission decision to begin researching scholarships. Some awards have deadlines before or shortly after college applications are due.

College students: keep searching

Scholarship opportunities do not necessarily end when a student starts college. Look for awards from the college, academic department, professional associations, community organizations, employers, and groups connected to the student's interests. Check whether awards are open to first-year, continuing, transfer, or graduate students.

A simple timeline to follow

  • Early high school: explore, record experiences, and learn where scholarships come from.
  • Junior year: identify likely opportunities and prepare application materials.
  • Senior year: apply consistently, track deadlines, and follow each sponsor’s instructions.
  • College: continue checking campus and outside scholarship opportunities.

Remember that deadlines vary

There is no single national scholarship deadline. Some awards open once a year, some have rolling deadlines, and others are tied to a school or program. Use the sponsor’s official website as the final source for dates and requirements.

Starting early gives students more time to find opportunities that fit and prepare thoughtful applications. The goal is steady progress, not doing everything at once.
